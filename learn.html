The things nobody sat you down and explained

The calculator gives you a number. This page explains every idea behind it — what each account actually does, why your tax bracket is not what you lose on the next dollar, and the unglamorous groundwork (an emergency fund, expensive debt) that comes before any of it. Written for people who have never been told how this works, not for the financially fluent.

Order of operations

Almost every question of the form "should I put money in X or Y?" has the same answer, and it is an order rather than a single account:

  • 1. Cover your minimum debt payments. Missing them costs more than any account earns, in interest and in credit damage.
  • 2. Build a small starter emergency fund. Without one, the first unexpected bill goes on a credit card and undoes everything else.
  • 3. Take the full employer match. A 50% match is an immediate 50% return. Nothing else on this page competes with that.
  • 4. Clear high-interest debt. Paying off a card charging 20% is a guaranteed 20% return. No investment offers a guaranteed 20%.
  • 5. Finish the emergency fund — three to six months of essential spending.
  • 6. Fill registered accounts in whatever order your own marginal rate justifies. That is the part the calculator works out for you.
Why the calculator can skip steps 1–5 It assumes you have already handled them, because it cannot see your debts or your cash cushion. If you have a credit card balance, the honest answer is that paying it off beats every allocation the calculator will suggest.
The registered accounts

"Registered" means registered with the Canada Revenue Agency for special tax treatment. None of them is an investment. Each is a container you put investments inside, and the container decides how the money is taxed. TFSA — Tax-Free Savings Account

Money goes in after tax; everything it earns is yours permanently. You can withdraw any amount, any time, for any reason, and the room comes back — but only on January 1 of the following year, which is where people accidentally over-contribute. Withdrawals do not count as income, so they never reduce income-tested benefits. That makes the TFSA the most flexible account in the system and the right default when you are unsure.

RRSP — Registered Retirement Savings Plan

A contribution is a deduction: it comes off your taxable income, so you get back your marginal rate on every dollar. You pay tax later, when you withdraw. The RRSP is therefore a bet that your rate in retirement will be lower than your rate today — and when it is not, the account actively loses you money.

At 71 an RRSP must be converted, usually to a RRIF, which forces a minimum withdrawal every year whether you want the income or not. Two withdrawal programmes let you take money out early without tax: the Home Buyers' Plan (up to $60,000 toward a first home, repayable) and the Lifelong Learning Plan for education.

Where an RRSP backfires On a modest income, RRSP and RRIF withdrawals in retirement count as income for the Guaranteed Income Supplement, which claws back roughly 50 cents on the dollar. Someone who contributed at a 20% marginal rate can face an effective 50%+ rate on the way out. The calculator refuses to recommend an RRSP in that situation, and says so.

FHSA — First Home Savings Account

The only account that is deductible going in and tax-free coming out, provided the money buys a qualifying first home. It is strictly better than an RRSP for anyone eligible, and badly under-used.

Unused room carries forward one year, which is why a single year can take $16,000. The account has a limited life: it must be closed by the end of the 15th year after opening, or the year you turn 71, or the year after your first qualifying withdrawal — whichever comes first. If you never buy a home, the balance can be rolled into an RRSP without using RRSP room, so opening one early costs you very little.

Why the projection changes after a few years At $8,000 a year the lifetime cap is reached in five full years. The calculator's chart shows government help stepping down when that happens, rather than pretending the deduction continues to retirement.

Withdrawals of growth and grant are taxed in the student's hands, which usually means little or no tax. Lower-income families may also qualify for the Canada Learning Bond, which pays into an RESP without any contribution at all.

Employer match and workplace pensions

If your employer contributes when you do, that is an immediate return no investment can match — a 50-cent match is 50% before the money is even invested. Take all of it first.

The match is not free of limits A group RRSP match uses the same contribution room your own deposits use, and both the employee and employer portions count. A defined-contribution pension instead generates a pension adjustment that reduces next year's RRSP room. Either way, room is consumed. Contributing past your limit is penalised at 1% per month on the excess above a $2,000 lifetime cushion, and the CRA charges the employee, not the employer.

Non-registered ("taxable") accounts

Once registered room is full, an ordinary investment account is still worth using — you simply pay tax on what it earns, and different income types are taxed differently. Capital gains are only partly taxable and only when you sell, eligible Canadian dividends receive a credit, and interest is taxed at your full rate. That ranking is why interest-paying investments are usually the first thing to shelter inside a TFSA or RRSP.

Contribution room

Every registered account has a limit, and the limits work differently:

  • RRSP room is earned. Each year you accumulate 18% of your previous year's earned income, up to $33,810, less any pension adjustment. Unused room carries forward indefinitely.
  • TFSA room is granted. Everyone 18 or older gets the same $7,000 regardless of income, it carries forward indefinitely, and withdrawals are restored the following calendar year.
  • FHSA room starts only when you open the account, which is why opening one early matters even if you cannot fund it yet.
Check before you contribute Your real numbers are in CRA My Account, and they are the only ones that count. The calculator estimates RRSP room from the income you type, which ignores carried-forward room and any pension adjustment — it can easily be out by thousands in either direction.
What goes inside the accounts

A TFSA is not an investment and neither is an RRSP. They are containers, and they grow at whatever you put inside them. Choosing the container is a tax decision; choosing the contents is an investment decision. The calculator handles the first and assumes the second.

The main things you can hold

  • Cash and GICs. Predictable, principal protected, and over long periods they have historically struggled to beat inflation. Right for money you need within a few years — an emergency fund, a down payment eighteen months out.
  • Bonds. Lending to a government or a company for interest. Steadier than stocks, lower expected return, and they lose value when interest rates rise.
  • Stocks (equities). Part-ownership of businesses. The highest expected long-run return and the highest short-run volatility. Falls of 30–50% have happened repeatedly and will happen again.
  • Everything else — real estate, commodities, crypto — carries its own risks and, in the case of crypto, no cash flow and no long history to reason from.

Risk is not one thing

Two different ideas share the word. Volatility is how much a price moves around; it is uncomfortable but it recovers. Permanent loss is capital that does not come back — a single company failing, or selling at the bottom and never buying back in. Broad diversification does very little about the first and a great deal about the second.

Your time horizon decides how much volatility you can carry. Money needed next year does not belong in stocks. Money for a retirement three decades away has time to sit through several full market cycles.

Index funds and ETFs

An index fund holds every company in a market in proportion to size, rather than trying to pick winners. An ETF — exchange-traded fund — is a fund that trades on an exchange like a stock; most of the widely held ones are index funds. A single broad ETF can hold thousands of companies across dozens of countries, which is diversification you could not assemble yourself at any reasonable cost.

Asset-allocation ETFs go one step further: a single fund holding a fixed mix of global stocks and bonds, rebalanced for you. One purchase, one ongoing decision, no spreadsheet. For most people the realistic alternative is not a better portfolio — it is no portfolio.

Fees compound exactly like returns

A fund's MER (management expense ratio) is charged every year on your whole balance, whether the fund gains or loses. It sounds trivial and is not.

$500 a month for 30 years at 7% before fees At a 0.2% MER you would finish with $563,612. At 2% — ordinary for an actively managed Canadian mutual fund — you would finish with $407,688. The difference is $155,925, on $180,000 of contributions. The fee gap is larger than a third of everything you put in. Computed with the same compounding the calculator uses.

Canada has historically had some of the highest mutual fund fees in the developed world. Ask what you are paying — the MER, plus any advice or trading fee on top. It is the one variable here you control completely.

Diversification, and the home-country trap

Canada is a small slice of the world's stock market — a low single-digit percentage — and it is heavily concentrated in financials and energy. A portfolio of only Canadian stocks is a bet on two sectors of one economy. Holding global equities is not exotic; it is the default that Canadian-only is a deviation from.

Asset location: which account holds what

Once you hold more than one account, where an investment sits changes its after-tax return.

  • The United States withholds tax on dividends paid to foreign investors. Under the Canada–US treaty an RRSP is exempt for US-listed US equities; a TFSA is not, and the withholding cannot be recovered.
  • Interest is taxed at your full marginal rate, so interest-paying investments benefit most from being sheltered.
  • Capital gains and eligible Canadian dividends are taxed more lightly, so they are the most tolerable things to hold in a non-registered account.
Worth knowing, not worth contorting for These rules are specific and depend on how a fund is listed and structured. Getting the contribution made matters far more than getting the location perfect. Check the details before restructuring anything.

Boring habits that do most of the work

  • Automate the contribution so it does not depend on how you feel that month.
  • Rebalance occasionally — once a year is plenty — back to your intended mix.
  • Do not try to time the market. Missing a handful of the best days, which cluster inside the worst periods, does lasting damage to a long-run return.
  • Expect declines and plan not to sell into them. The largest avoidable risk in a long-horizon portfolio is the investor's own behaviour in a bad year.
The risks of picking individual stocks

Buying shares in individual companies is the version of investing that gets talked about, and it carries risks that a broad fund does not.

Company-specific risk pays you nothing

Risk splits into two parts. Market risk is the whole market moving; you cannot diversify it away, and bearing it is what long-run returns compensate you for. Company-specific risk is one firm losing a lawsuit, a patent, or a chief executive. It can be diversified away almost for free — so the market does not pay you a premium for carrying it. Holding five stocks instead of five thousand adds risk without adding expected return.

Most stocks are not the market

Long-run studies of stock returns keep finding the same shape: a small minority of companies account for nearly all of the market's gain over decades, while the typical stock underperforms and a large share lose money outright. An index owns the winners automatically. A concentrated portfolio has to find them, and the base rate for doing so is unfavourable.

This is also why hindsight is so misleading. Every enormous winner is obvious afterwards, and the companies that looked equally promising and failed are not the ones anybody writes about.

Employer shares are a double exposure If your savings are concentrated in your employer's stock, your income and your portfolio depend on one company. The bad scenario takes both at once, which is exactly when you can least afford it. Discounted share-purchase plans can still be worth taking — selling and diversifying afterwards is usually the point.

Who is on the other side of your trade

Every time you buy, somebody with a view is selling. Often that somebody is a full-time institution with faster data and lower costs. That does not make picking stocks impossible, but it should set expectations: you are competing, not simply participating.

Speculation is a separate category

  • Leverage and margin magnify losses as well as gains and can force a sale at the worst possible moment.
  • Options can expire worthless — a total loss on that position is a normal outcome, not a freak one.
  • Crypto and meme stocks have no earnings to anchor a valuation; prices rest on what the next buyer will pay.
  • Anything promoted to you — a hot tip, a message from a stranger, a guaranteed return — deserves the assumption that you are the exit. Check registration through the CSA's National Registration Search before sending money anywhere.
Two Canadian traps Frequent trading inside a TFSA can be treated by the CRA as carrying on a business, making the profits fully taxable — the shelter is not unconditional. And capital losses in a TFSA or RRSP cannot be claimed against gains elsewhere, so a losing position in a registered account loses twice.

If you want to do it anyway

Plenty of people find individual stocks genuinely interesting, and interest is worth something if it keeps you engaged with your own finances. The common compromise is to keep the long-term plan in broad, low-cost funds and to size individual positions as a small, deliberately capped share of the total — an amount you could lose entirely without changing your retirement.

Where the calculator stands It assumes a single steady rate of return and takes no view on what you hold. It cannot tell you whether your portfolio is diversified, what you are paying in fees, or whether an 8% assumption is reasonable for your mix. Those are yours to check.
Your real marginal rate

Canada taxes income in brackets, and only the income inside each bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate. Earning one dollar into a higher bracket never reduces your take-home pay — the "I got a raise and lost money" story is almost always about something else on this page.

Your marginal rate is what you lose on the next dollar. Your posted bracket is only part of it, because income-tested benefits shrink as income rises, and a shrinking benefit is indistinguishable from a tax.

The Canada Child Benefit

The CCB pays up to $8,157 a year for a child under 6 and $6,883 for a child aged 6 to 17. It is reduced once adjusted family net income passes $38,237, at a rate that depends on how many children you have, then at a lower rate above $82,847.

Those percentages stack on top of income tax. A three-child family in the first band loses 19% of every extra dollar in benefit plus their income tax — which is how a household whose posted bracket says 28.2% actually keeps barely half of a raise.

The BC Family Benefit

A provincial benefit on top of the CCB: up to $1,750 for a first child, $1,100 for a second and $900 for each additional one, plus up to $500 for a single parent. It reduces at 4% of AFNI above $30,176 — but only down to a floor ($775 for the first child). The floor then holds, costing nothing at the margin, until $96,562, above which it reduces at 4% again.

Why the shape matters That is two clawback bands with a flat plateau between them. Your BCFB marginal cost is 4%, then nothing, then 4% again. A raise that feels identical can cost very different amounts depending on which band you are in.

The Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit

The renamed GST/HST credit: up to $679 for a single person, $890 for a couple, plus $234 per child. Note the age limit is under 19, not the CCB's under-18. It reduces at 5% of AFNI above $46,432, so it adds 5% to the effective marginal rate across that band.

Not independently verified The BCFB and CGEB figures on this page were supplied to the project rather than taken from a CRA publication the maintainers checked. Confirm them before relying on the numbers.

The BC tax reduction

British Columbia has a low-income credit worth up to $690 that phases out at 3.56% of income above $25,570. That adds 3.56% to the effective marginal rate of everyone in the phase-out band, and it appears in no bracket table anywhere.

Why this makes contributions worth more

A deduction runs the whole mechanism in reverse. Lowering your taxable income returns the tax and restores the benefit you were losing. For a family in a clawback band, the benefit restored can exceed the tax refund itself — which is exactly what the calculator's "what the government gives back" figure adds up.

CPP and EI are deliberately excluded They are levied on gross employment income and an RRSP deduction does not reduce them, so including them would overstate what a contribution is worth. For reference, 2026 rates are 5.95% CPP on earnings between $3,500 and $74,600, 4% on the slice up to $85,000, and 1.63% EI up to $68,900.
Credits and deductions

These two words get used interchangeably and mean different things.

  • A deduction reduces the income you are taxed on. It is worth your marginal rate: the same contribution saves a high earner more than a low earner, because they get back a larger share of it.
  • A non-refundable credit reduces the tax you owe, calculated at the lowest rate — federally 14%, and 5.6% in BC. It is worth the same to everyone, and it cannot take your tax below zero.
  • A refundable credit is paid to you even if you owe no tax at all. The GST/HST credit and the Canada Workers Benefit work this way, which is why filing a return matters even on a very low income.

The basic personal amount

Everyone gets a slice of income effectively tax-free through a non-refundable credit. Federally the amount is $14,829, plus a supplement of up to $1,623 that phases out between $181,440 and $258,482. British Columbia's is $13,216.

Credits worth knowing about

  • Canada Workers Benefit — refundable, for lower-income workers.
  • GST/HST credit — refundable, paid quarterly, income-tested. Like the CCB it phases out, so it adds to the effective marginal rate in its band.
  • Disability tax credit — substantial, transferable to a supporting relative, and a gateway to the Registered Disability Savings Plan.
  • Medical expenses — claimable above a threshold based on your income, and usually best claimed by the lower-income spouse.
  • Tuition — carried forward or transferred to a parent or grandparent.
  • Charitable donations, pension income, age amount, caregiver amounts — each with its own rules.
  • Spousal transfers — many credits move to a partner when the first person cannot use them.
Dollar amounts deliberately omitted Only the figures this project sources and version-controls are quoted with numbers on this page. The credits above change most years and several are province-specific; check current amounts on canada.ca rather than trusting a figure memorised from a blog post.
CPP, OAS and GIS

Government retirement income covers the first slice of what you need, so your savings only have to cover the rest. Calculators that ignore it make the target look terrifying; calculators that assume the maximum make it look too easy.

CPP — Canada Pension Plan

A pension you build by working and contributing. The maximum at 65 assumes roughly 39 years of contributions at the earnings ceiling, which is why the average new pension is far below it — the calculator uses the average deliberately. Starting before 65 permanently reduces it; starting later permanently increases it. CPP is taxable income.

OAS — Old Age Security

Paid from 65 based on how long you have lived in Canada, not on whether you worked. Full residency is 40 years after 18; less is prorated. Payments increase at 75. OAS is taxable and is clawed back above an income threshold — one of the few places where a high retirement income genuinely reduces a benefit.

GIS — Guaranteed Income Supplement

A non-taxable top-up for low-income seniors receiving OAS, reduced sharply as other income rises — roughly 50 cents per dollar. Because RRSP and RRIF withdrawals count as income and TFSA withdrawals do not, the choice between those two accounts matters enormously for anyone likely to receive GIS.

Budgeting

The calculator asks what you can save each month. Working that out honestly is the hardest input on the page, and the one with the most leverage.

Start from what actually left your account

Not what you think you spend — three months of statements. Almost everyone discovers a subscription they forgot and a category twice the size they assumed.

Three buckets

  • Fixed — rent or mortgage, insurance, utilities, transit, debt minimums. Hard to change quickly, and the biggest wins hide here.
  • Variable — groceries, fuel, everything discretionary. Easy to change, usually smaller than people expect.
  • Savings — treated as a bill you pay yourself, not as whatever is left over. Nothing is ever left over.

A common starting split is roughly half your take-home to needs, thirty percent to wants, twenty percent to savings and debt. Treat it as a sanity check, not a rule — it is unreachable in an expensive housing market, and unambitious on a high income.

Automate it

Move savings the day you are paid. A transfer that happens automatically survives a bad week; one that depends on willpower at month-end does not. Increase the amount whenever your pay rises, before you adjust to the higher income.

Irregular income If you are self-employed or on variable hours, budget from your lowest recent month and treat anything above it as a windfall to allocate deliberately. Also set aside tax and CPP as the money arrives — nobody withholds it for you.
The emergency fund

Cash you can reach today, for the things that actually happen: a job loss, a car repair, a flight home. Its job is not to earn — it is to stop a bad month becoming credit card debt that takes years to clear.

How much

  • Starter: one month of essential spending, or around a thousand dollars. Enough to absorb an ordinary shock.
  • Full: three to six months of essential spending — the fixed bucket, not your whole lifestyle.
  • More if your income is variable, you are the only earner, or you work in a field where finding the next job takes months.

Where to keep it

Somewhere boring and immediately accessible: a high-interest savings account, or a TFSA holding cash or a money-market fund. This is the one case where cash in a TFSA is the right answer. Do not put it in stocks — the moment you need it is disproportionately likely to be a moment when markets are also down.

Using a TFSA for this Withdrawing restores your room, but not until January 1 of the following year. If you empty it in March and refill it in November, you have over-contributed and will be charged 1% a month on the excess.
Debt

Paying off debt is an investment with a guaranteed, tax-free return equal to its interest rate. Compare that rate against what you could reasonably expect to earn — the calculator defaults to 8% a year, before tax, and with no guarantee whatsoever. A credit card at 20% wins that comparison every time.

A rough ranking

  • Above ~10% — payday loans, credit cards, most store cards. Clear these before investing anything beyond an employer match.
  • Roughly 5–10% — car loans, unsecured lines of credit, some student debt. A close call. Splitting between the debt and investing is defensible.
  • Below ~5% — most mortgages, subsidised student loans. Paying the minimum and investing the difference is usually the better expected outcome, though paying down debt is the certain one.

Two ways to attack multiple debts

  • Highest rate first (avalanche) — mathematically optimal; costs the least in total interest.
  • Smallest balance first (snowball) — costs slightly more, but clears individual debts sooner. The method you actually stick with beats the one that is theoretically better.

Canadian specifics

  • Interest on student loans may qualify for a non-refundable credit; interest on consumer debt does not.
  • Interest on money borrowed to earn investment income can be deductible, with strict tracing rules. Get advice before relying on that.
  • Consolidating debt only helps if the new rate is genuinely lower and the freed-up room does not get spent again.
  • A mortgage is not an emergency fund. Money paid into it is difficult to get back at exactly the moment you would need it most.
Compound growth

Growth earns growth. That is the entire idea, and it is why the calculator's chart bends upward instead of running straight.

The chart splits the balance into what you contributed, the government help reinvested, and the growth on both. Early on, almost all of it is your own money — which is why the first years feel like nothing is happening. The point where the growth band overtakes everything you have put in is marked on the chart, and after it your investments are contributing more each year than you are.

This is why starting earlier beats saving harder later: a dollar invested at 25 has forty years to compound, and a dollar invested at 55 has ten. The calculator's "cost of waiting" figure is that difference, and it is always larger than people expect, because the years you skip are the ones with the most compounding left.

A flat rate is a simplification Real returns arrive unevenly. A 30-year average of 8% can contain several years of losses, and the order in which good and bad years arrive matters a great deal near retirement. This model uses one steady rate and no market crashes.
Future dollars

The calculator reports balances in future dollars — the actual number that would appear on a statement, with no inflation adjustment.

Keep in mind what that means. Prices rise, so a sum decades away will not buy what the same sum buys now. At 2% inflation, purchasing power roughly halves over 35 years. A seven-figure balance in forty years is a real number, but it is not a forty-year-from-now fortune in today's terms.

One comparison to treat carefully In Are you on track?, CPP and OAS are shown at today's payment rates while your projected portfolio income is in future dollars. Both pensions are indexed in reality, so the two sides of that comparison sit on different footings. Read the split between them as indicative rather than exact.
What this does not model

Being clear about the edges is part of being trustworthy about the middle.

  • British Columbia only. Other provinces have their own brackets, credits and child benefits. Quebec is a separate system entirely.
  • BCFB and CGEB parameters are not independently verified. They are modelled, and recorded in the tax-data file as user-supplied. Confirm them against CRA and BC publications before relying on the output.
  • GIS and the OAS clawback are flagged by the guardrails but not modelled, so retirement-age scenarios are incomplete.
  • One flat rate of return, no market volatility, no sequence-of-returns risk.
  • Contribution room is estimated from the income you type. Your real room is in CRA My Account.
  • No self-employment, rental, capital-gains or dividend income, and none of the credits listed above beyond the basic personal amounts.
